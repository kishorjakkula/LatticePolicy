# How P&C underwriters really decide risk

Property & Casualty underwriting is not a single moment of judgment — it is a layered pipeline of triage, data-chasing, rules-based gating, and escalation in which most policies never touch a human underwriter at all, while the ones that do generate a documented, referral-stamped paper trail built as much for a future E&O defense as for pricing accuracy. Personal lines and small commercial risk increasingly resolve through **straight-through processing (STP)** with no manual touch, while mid-market and large commercial accounts remain "assisted" rather than automated: software surfaces loss-run patterns, financial ratios, and catastrophe scores, but a human underwriter still owns the accept/price/terms decision and must document why ([hyperexponential](https://www.hyperexponential.com/blog/agentic-ai-insurance-underwriting)). Underwriting authority is not a single dollar figure per person but a multi-axis grid — premium, limit, class, territory, and transaction type stacked together — enforced less through the written guideline manual than through hard-coded referral triggers that automatically escalate anything outside a pre-set threshold ([Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/underwriting-authority); [Guidewire](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/pc/202507/cloudapibf/cloudAPI/topics/124-PCsupport/00-underwriting-issues/c_referral_reasons.html)). That same appetite logic plays out at a macro level: when carriers retrench from catastrophe-exposed property — as roughly a dozen major insurers did in California and Florida between 2021 and 2025 — the displaced risk does not disappear, it lands in state FAIR plans and residual-market pools that ballooned to nearly **$700 billion of exposure in California alone by late 2025** ([Carrier Management](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2025/02/10/271636.htm)). Underwriting profitability swung sharply over the research window too: the US P&C industry posted its best combined ratio in over a decade in 2025 (around 92–93) even as reinsurance pricing — which directly dictates how much risk a primary carrier must retain, and therefore how tightly it underwrites — flipped from the hardest catastrophe reinsurance market since 1992 in January 2023 to double-digit rate declines by mid-2026 ([AM Best via Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/02/24/859393.htm); [Artemis.bm](https://www.artemis.bm/news/property-cat-reinsurance-down-14-7-retrocession-down-16-5-at-jan-2026-renewals-howden-re/)). What follows traces that pipeline end to end: how a submission gets triaged and investigated, how appetite and authority are actually operationalized, what tools and data sources underwriters lean on (including the real, verifiable state of AI-assisted underwriting), how renewal underwriting differs from new business, how the underwriting cycle and reinsurance terms feed back into day-to-day risk selection, and where the system still visibly breaks.

## Triage, clearance, and the four data sources that actually drive a file forward

A submission does not go straight to an underwriter's desk — it passes through three distinct, sequential gates that a workbench architecture treats as separable: **intake** (normalizing raw email/portal/ACORD documents into structured data and checking completeness), **clearance** (duplicate, eligibility, conflict, and compliance checks — "should we even look at this?"), and **triage** (scoring cleared submissions by profitability likelihood and routing them to the right underwriter or straight through automation) ([hyperexponential](https://www.hyperexponential.com/blog/submission-triage-insurance)). Clearance matters because it screens for appetite fit *before* any underwriting time is spent — Guidewire's UnderwritingCenter workbench, for example, performs broker license validation and sanctions checks at this earliest stage specifically to "accelerate triage and clearance to help underwriters quickly identify out-of-appetite risks" ([Guidewire](https://www.guidewire.com/products/core-products/insurancesuite/underwritingcenter-insurance-underwriting-software)). The design logic is deliberate: rules-driven automation handles both ends of the risk distribution — the clearly in-appetite and the clearly out-of-appetite — while human judgment is reserved only for the borderline middle ([hyperexponential](https://www.hyperexponential.com/blog/submission-triage-insurance)).

Once a file clears into active underwriting, four distinct data sources answer four distinct questions, and underwriters read each one differently. **Loss runs** answer "how has this risk actually behaved" — and the single most important discipline here is distinguishing frequency from severity: "a $150,000 total from one freak auto loss prices better than the same total spread across eighteen slip-and-fall claims," because frequency signals an uncorrected operational problem while an isolated severe loss often reads as bad luck ([Menlo Insurance](https://www.menloinsurance.com/library/guides/how-to-read-loss-runs)). Underwriters track three valuations per claim — paid, reserved, and incurred (paid plus reserved) — and treat the reserve figure as the most scrutinized number on the page, because "every reserve dollar is a premium dollar" and stale reserves are the single biggest red flag in a loss run; a loss run older than 90 days is typically discounted or bounced outright, since a stale $60,000 reserve that should actually be $9,000 "can mean the difference between a standard market placement and a surplus lines quote" ([Menlo Insurance](https://www.menloinsurance.com/library/guides/how-to-read-loss-runs)). Underwriters generally want five years of loss history before making commercial decisions, and declining frequency year-over-year plus a clean recent 12–24 months reads as a strong positive signal ([Wexford Insurance](https://www.wexfordins.com/post/why-underwriters-want-five-years-of-loss-history); [Vouch](https://www.vouch.us/blog/what-is-a-loss-run-report)).

**Financial statements** answer "can this business absorb a loss" — underwriters examine the current ratio, debt-to-worth/leverage, and tangible net worth to gauge liquidity and staying power, with errors, an unbalanced balance sheet, or late-filed statements standing out as red flags ([NASBP](https://www.nasbp.org/post/what-do-commercial-surety-underwriters-look-for-in-financial-statements/)). Notably, this balance-sheet analysis matters most for commercial property and liability risk selection on larger accounts; workers' compensation pricing instead leans almost entirely on the bureau-calculated **Experience Modification Factor (E-Mod)**, built from the insured's own three-year loss history relative to its classification's expected losses — which is one reason workers' comp posted a strong 91% combined ratio industry-wide in 2025 even as GL ran an underwriting loss ([NCRB](https://www.ncrb.org/modcalc/Instruction.aspx); [Risk & Insurance](https://riskandinsurance.com/workers-compensation-remains-profitable-as-premium-dips-and-severity-climbs/)). **Property inspections** answer "what is the physical hazard" — commercial inspections commonly cover the "four points" (roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical), and findings directly drive outcomes: significant hazards can trigger an outright decline, a pricing surcharge, or a mandatory mitigation condition before coverage binds ([CCPIA](https://ccpia.org/commercial-property-insurance-inspections/)). The industry is visibly shifting this step away from mandatory on-site visits toward vendor-supplied roof-condition and hazard scores (e.g., Verisk's ProMetrix) layered alongside ISO's community-level Public Protection Classification (fire-protection rating), with physical inspections now often reserved for higher-value or flagged properties ([Verisk ProMetrix](https://www.verisk.com/products/prometrix/roof-underwriting-report/); [Texas DOI](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/fire/fmppcfaq.html)). Finally, **CLUE/A-PLUS and MVR databases** answer "what is this specific individual's history" for personal lines — CLUE reports carry up to seven years of personal-auto and home claims history, while an MVR flags violations, accidents, and license status, with a known data-completeness gap: a violation in one state can lag before appearing on another state's record ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/credit-reports-and-scores/consumer-reporting-companies/companies-list/comprehensive-loss-underwriting-exchange/); [Progressive](https://www.progressive.com/answers/what-is-a-motor-vehicle-record/)). Across all four sources, data *freshness* is the recurring operational hazard — stale reserves, outdated financials, lagging MVR updates — more than any single data point being wrong.

## Classification, pricing, and the decision menu beyond accept-or-decline

Underwriting guidelines sort a cleared risk into preferred, standard, or substandard — and on top of that mechanical classification, underwriters apply **schedule rating**, an explicitly judgment-based credit or debit for risk characteristics that resist quantification: safety programs, management practices, equipment condition, operational controls ([Wexford Insurance](https://www.wexfordins.com/post/experience-rating-vs-schedule-rating-underwriters-price-business)). This is a meaningful distinction for anyone modeling the process: classification is meant to be applied consistently and mechanically, while schedule rating is meant to capture individual underwriter judgment about the parts of a risk that classification rules can't reach — conflating the two blurs an audit distinction carriers care about.

Pricing and the accept/decline decision are far more interleaved than a clean two-step process. The commonly described commercial sequence runs submission receipt → clearance/appetite screening → extraction and enrichment → **risk assessment and pricing as a combined stage** → referral and authority checks → quote and negotiation → bind and issuance ([Nomad Data](https://www.nomad-data.com/blog/underwriting-submission-triage-ai)). In practice, "rated risks within authority go straight to quote and risks outside authority get a referral package" — meaning the pricing output itself is one of the inputs that determines whether a file needs escalation before it can even become a quote ([oipinsurtech](https://www.oipinsurtech.com/reimagining-the-end%E2%80%91to%E2%80%91end-underwriting-process/)). Underwriters retain ownership of risk selection and pricing *together*, "both because commercial risk demands it and because supervisors increasingly expect a documented human decision point" ([hyperexponential](https://www.hyperexponential.com/blog/agentic-ai-insurance-underwriting)), and even at the final bind stage, modern workbenches like Guidewire UnderwritingCenter rerun validations and reconfirm premium and authority limits one more time before issuance — rating is never fully "settled" until the moment of bind.

The accept/decline binary undersells the actual range of outcomes. Underwriters can approve as submitted, approve with modifications (higher premium, added exclusions, a higher deductible, reduced limits), postpone pending more information, refer upward, or decline outright. A particularly important middle category is the **subjectivity** — a specific condition that must be satisfied before (or sometimes after) binding, such as a signed inspection report or proof of completed repairs; most carriers designate certain subjectivities as "pre-bind critical," meaning an open one blocks the bind action entirely, while others function as ongoing conditions of continued coverage whose non-satisfaction can invalidate cover mid-term ([Total CSR Glossary](https://totalcsr.com/insurance-glossary/subjectivities-a-k-a-contingencies/); [BriteCore](https://help.britecore.com/hc/en-us/articles/41684971681939-Subjectivities)).

Every one of these decisions generates a documentation obligation that serves two purposes simultaneously. It is the primary defense in an **E&O claim** — lack of proper documentation makes it difficult to later prove a client provided misleading application information, and best practice dictates that any underwriting exception be documented in writing in the file ([CoverDesk](https://coverdesk.com/documentation-lowering-errors-and-omissions-exposure/); [Agents Alliance](https://agentsalliance.com/insurance-agent-eo-issues-in-underwriting-and-binding-procedures/)). It is also a regulatory requirement: under NAIC market-conduct examination standards, a policy file must contain enough detail that an examiner can "readily ascertain" compliance and *reconstruct* — not just confirm the outcome of — the rating and underwriting that produced the policy, with records generally retained for the current year plus three (sometimes five) years ([NAIC Market Regulation Handbook](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/prod_serv_marketreg_mes_hb.pdf)). Because the regulatory standard is reconstruction, not just outcome-recording, any system of record effectively needs point-in-time snapshots of which guideline version applied to a given historical decision, not merely today's current rules.

Underlying all of this is the sharpest operational divide in the business: **personal lines vs. commercial lines**. Personal risk is standardized enough that up to 95% of policies at leading carriers move through full STP with zero underwriter involvement, built on consistent rating factors, credit-based insurance scores, and CLUE/MVR lookups; commercial risk is heterogeneous enough — "a restaurant, a manufacturer, and a tech startup have utterly different exposures" — that even small-business underwriting, which is highly automated, still routes complex characteristics to a human, while large/complex commercial accounts remain deliberately bespoke, expert-driven, and resistant to full automation ([Capco](https://www.capco.com/intelligence/capco-intelligence/bionic-underwriting)). The practical strategic model carriers have converged on is to calibrate automation by segment — algorithms absorb routine, standardized risk, and underwriter talent concentrates on the accounts complex enough to need it — rather than deploying one undifferentiated workflow across both lines.

## Appetite is enforced by rules, not reminders — and its edges land in FAIR plans

Carrier underwriting manuals are structured as tiered rulebooks: a top-level appetite statement, then line-of-business eligibility lists splitting classes into eligible / ineligible / refer-to-underwriter, then granular "red flag" triggers — specific hazards, loss-history thresholds, construction details — that force escalation or an automatic decline ([MMG Commercial Lines Underwriting Guidelines](https://www.mmgins.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/MMG-CL-Underwriting-Guidelines-2023.pdf)). Concrete examples recur across contractor GL, builders-risk, and restaurant appetite guides: ground-up residential construction and torch-down roofing are commonly excluded from contractor GL even when general contracting itself is eligible; tabletop cooking formats (hibachi, Korean BBQ, fondue) and 24-hour convenience-store operations are frequently ineligible; heavy chemical manufacturing and foundries are commonly avoided regardless of a carrier's general appetite for "manufacturers" ([BusinessInsuranceUSA](https://www.businessinsuranceusa.com/blog/insurance/contractor-general-liability-exclusions-by-trade/)). The pattern that recurs across every manual reviewed is that "in appetite" is never a single binary attribute of the applicant — it is the intersection of a base segment (classification code, construction type, driver class) with disqualifying hazard details layered on top, so a risk can be in-appetite at the segment level and still be referred or declined because of one specific attribute.

A persistent, well-documented pain point is that this appetite logic still mostly lives in **static documents** rather than enforced systems — "appetite lives in PDFs, Word documents, and the working memory of senior underwriters," which directly undermines the premise that a portfolio's loss ratio reflects every decision being measured against the same risk appetite ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/industry-analyses/how-to-analyze-a-property-casualty-insurance/risk-appetite-alignment-and-underwriting-discipline-analysis/)). The emerging — not yet universal — remedy is a centralized digital appetite platform that lets a carrier input guidelines once and expose them consistently to agents and underwriters alike (named examples include Ask Kodiak and the Ivans Distribution Platform) ([IVANS](https://www.ivans.com/for-carriers/solutions/appetite-eligibility/)); in the meantime, referral triggers remain the most reliable consistency control precisely because they are binary and procedural — a limit is either exceeded or not — rather than a matter of interpreting open-ended appetite language.

Appetite also has a hard regulatory floor in certain lines: the **"take-all-comers" (or "all-comers") rule**. Michigan's Essential Insurance Act requires that "every applicant or insured conforming with the underwriting rules will be insured or renewed, and every applicant or insured not conforming with the underwriting rules will be refused" — the insurer keeps full discretion over what its *filed* rules say, but loses discretion over whether to apply those rules uniformly once filed ([Michigan Legislature, MCL 500.2119](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-500-2119)). California achieves a functionally equivalent constraint for "good drivers" under Prop. 103-derived statute, barring insurers from refusing a Good Driver Discount policy to anyone meeting the statutory criteria and explicitly prohibiting "absence of prior insurance" as a disqualifier ([Cal. Ins. Code §1861.02](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/insurance-code/ins-sect-1861-02/)). Ontario's FSRA operates the clearest version of this mechanism, and its own 2023 thematic review found "intentional non-compliance industry-wide," with insurers using non-filed, informal practices to quietly steer away from certain consumer types rather than openly violating the rule — a telling illustration that shifting control from case-by-case decisions to rule-filing doesn't eliminate underwriters' incentive to discriminate, it just pushes that incentive into less visible channels ([FSRA Thematic Review](https://www.fsrao.ca/industry/auto-insurance/publications/take-all-comers-thematic-review-report)).

When private appetite retrenches entirely from a geography — as it has for wildfire and hurricane risk since 2022 — the displaced risk does not vanish; it lands in a state **FAIR plan**, the "insurer of last resort" mechanism operating in 33 states plus DC, funded by all admitted carriers roughly in proportion to their voluntary market share ([NAIC CIPR](https://content.naic.org/cipr-topics/fair-access-insurance-requirements-plans)). The scale of this shift over 2023–2025 is the single clearest quantifiable signal of appetite retrenchment available: California's FAIR Plan grew 276% in policy count from 2018–2024, with exposure reaching roughly **$700 billion and about 650,000 policies in force by September 2025**, overtaking Florida's Citizens as the largest residual property market in the country ([Carrier Management](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2025/02/10/271636.htm); [UPHelp](https://uphelp.org/californias-fair-plan-carries-growing-load-as-insurers-retreat-beyond-wildfire-zones/)). Because every admitted carrier in the state is assessed for FAIR plan losses in proportion to its own voluntary market share, a single carrier's decision to decline a wildfire-exposed property doesn't fully insulate that carrier from the risk — it remains indirectly on the hook through FAIR plan assessments. Florida and Texas illustrate two further design variants: Florida's Citizens runs an active "Depopulation Program" where a private takeout offer within 20% of the Citizens renewal premium forces the policyholder off the public plan, while homes valued above $700,000 (or $1 million in Miami-Dade/Monroe) are ineligible for Citizens altogether ([Citizens Property Insurance](https://www.citizensfla.com/depopulation)); Texas instead splits residual coverage by peril and geography, routing windstorm/hail risk in 14 first-tier coastal counties exclusively to the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association while the statewide Texas FAIR Plan covers fire/theft/other perils everywhere else ([TDI TWIA Overview](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/pc/twia-overview.pdf)).

## Authority cascades in a grid, and a referral is a packaged argument

Underwriting authority is granted through a formal written instrument — an underwriting authority letter for an employee, or a binding authority agreement for a delegated third party — that scopes power to bind along several axes simultaneously: maximum premium or limit, permitted lines/classes, territory, and transaction type ([Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/underwriting-authority)). A representative real-world clause permits an underwriter "to offer property and casualty coverage... only up to a cap of $1M but not beyond" for "retail clothing businesses, but not to a construction business whose daily operations are considerably more hazardous" — premium caps and class restrictions arrive together in the same grant, not as separate rules ([Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/underwriting-authority)). Some carriers choose not to delegate at all — "United reserves to itself all underwriting authority" is a standard contractual option alongside delegation, confirming that delegation sits on a spectrum rather than being an all-or-nothing default ([Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/underwriting-authority)). Crucially, authority is re-evaluated at every transaction, not spent once at inception: policy administration systems like Guidewire PolicyCenter attach "referral reasons" directly to the policy so that a new job — a renewal or an endorsement — automatically re-triggers the underwriting-issue evaluation, meaning a policy within authority at new business can still generate a referral later if a renewal or endorsement pushes it past a threshold ([Guidewire Cloud API docs](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/pc/202507/cloudapibf/cloudAPI/topics/124-PCsupport/00-underwriting-issues/c_referral_reasons.html)).

Authority scales with seniority — associate underwriters work within narrow assigned limits and prep renewals for the underwriter above them; senior underwriters (often 8+ years' experience) handle more complex or higher-risk applications and help set strategy; chief underwriters sit at the top, deciding the most unusual risks ([search synthesis via insuedot.com](https://www.insuedot.com/insurance-glossary/insurance-glossary-underwriting-authority/)) — but this scaling is genuinely two-dimensional rather than a strict ladder: a specialist in a narrow class like environmental liability or cyber can hold elevated authority within that niche independent of their general seniority tier, and authority itself is revisited over time based on a track record of sound decisions, not fixed permanently at hire ([insuedot.com synthesis](https://www.insuedot.com/insurance-glossary/insurance-glossary-underwriting-authority/)). No public source gave verifiable, carrier-specific dollar bands by seniority tier — these figures are treated as proprietary across the industry.

A **referral** is never a bare request for sign-off. Operationally, a referral attaches a specific "referral reason" or "underwriting issue" to the policy; money-type referrals require a quantified amount and currency, not just narrative, and escalation documentation is expected to walk through each specific guideline breach step by step, recording the date, the escalating and receiving parties, and any interim actions taken ([Guidewire docs](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/pc/202507/cloudapibf/cloudAPI/topics/124-PCsupport/00-underwriting-issues/c_referral_reasons.html); [Neutrinos synthesis](https://marketplace.neutrinos.com/asset/underwriting-referral-management)). Systems typically distinguish a standard "/close" (resolved through normal approval) from a "/special-close" requiring elevated permission to clear a referral without going through standard approval — operationalizing the idea that only someone at the highest authority tier can unilaterally override certain referrals ([Guidewire docs](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/pc/202507/cloudapibf/cloudAPI/topics/124-PCsupport/00-underwriting-issues/c_referral_reasons.html)). No source in this research surfaced a concrete, citable SLA figure (e.g., "24 hours" or "3 business days") for referral turnaround at a named carrier — this appears to be treated as internal, unpublicized operational policy industry-wide.

Authority cascades contractually down the chain: carrier → MGA/MGU (Lloyd's calls these "coverholders") → retail agent, via a binding authority agreement or Terms of Business Agreement, which may itself be sub-delegated with further restrictions to retail producers (though this third link — MGA-to-retail-agent sub-delegation — is comparatively undocumented in public sources). Under the NAIC's Managing General Agents Act (Model #225), an entity becomes a regulated MGA, triggering the Act's on-site-review requirement, once it underwrites gross direct written premium equal to or more than **5% of the insurer's policyholder surplus** in any quarter or year — a bright-line financial-exposure threshold ([NAIC Model Act #225](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/model-law-225.pdf)). Oversight runs well beyond periodic bordereaux review: NAIC guidance recommends auditing a minimum of 5% of MGA-bound policies annually, with best-practice carriers auditing 10–15%, and AM Best's 2025 rating criteria explicitly weighs demonstrated oversight of delegated authority in a carrier's financial-strength assessment ([insurnest.com synthesis](https://insurnest.com/agent-details/insurance/analytics/delegated-underwriting-audit-ai-agent-in-analytics-for-mga-operations/)). Lloyd's publishes the most detailed version of this oversight regime: new coverholders are audited within their first year, ongoing frequency is risk-scored off binder size, coverholder history, and bordereaux intelligence, and unannounced "for-cause" audits can be triggered by customer complaints, claims-ratio deviations, or management turnover ([getregure.com](https://www.getregure.com/blog/lloyds-delegated-authority-audit-requirements/)). Lloyd's audit standard explicitly "does not accept oral assurances" — findings are graded from minor observation through significant (requiring a remediation plan) to critical, and critical findings can trigger reduced authority limits, suspension of new business, or outright termination; Lloyd's also has a formal mid-term step between full authority and termination — reducing a coverholder to **"prior submit" status**, where every risk must be individually approved before binding, rather than being bound autonomously ([getregure.com](https://www.getregure.com/blog/lloyds-delegated-authority-audit-requirements/)).

Exceeding authority has a consistent two-track consequence pattern. The policyholder relationship is generally honored first — a documented real-world case shows an agent binding coverage without obtaining required underwriting documentation; the insurer still paid the resulting claim, then separately sought reimbursement from the agent ([Agents Alliance](https://agentsalliance.com/insurance-agent-eo-issues-in-underwriting-and-binding-procedures/)). Internally, consequences scale with whether the breach was properly escalated: Lloyd's treats exceeding a limit as a *critical* audit finding specifically "if exceeded without syndicate referral" — meaning the real violation is binding outside authority *without* escalating, not merely encountering a risk above one's threshold ([getregure.com](https://www.getregure.com/blog/lloyds-delegated-authority-audit-requirements/)). Termination of Lloyd's delegated authority is characterized as "a serious market event" reported to the FCA that damages the party's prospects with other syndicates — a reputational consequence well beyond the single relationship affected.

## The underwriter's toolkit: loss runs, cat models, credit scores, and the real state of AI

Beyond the four core data sources already described, two tools shape property underwriting specifically. **Catastrophe models** (AIR/Verisk Touchstone, RMS/Moody's, and peril-specific tools like Zesty.ai and Guidewire HazardHub) have migrated from pure portfolio/reinsurance aggregation tools into point-of-underwriting decisioning: AIR's Touchstone added a dedicated "Underwriting Mode" whose dashboard flags individual locations that violate a carrier's risk tolerance at the moment of submission, and HazardHub-style platforms can pre-populate a submission with **1,200+ individual property risk factors** translated into expected-loss damage functions ([Carrier Management](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2014/07/15/126114.htm); [Guidewire HazardHub](https://www.guidewire.com/products/analytics/hazardhub-risk-data)). A notable dissenting industry voice should be weighed against this consensus: one vendor commentary argues cat models were designed for portfolio-level aggregation and are being misapplied when relied on too heavily for individual-risk accept/decline decisions ([Intermap](https://www.intermap.com/risks-of-hazard-blog/2017/03/cat-models-are-not-for-underwriting)) — a real methodological tension between speed/automation and the models' original design intent. **Credit-based insurance scores** — a FICO-style score built from payment history (40%), outstanding debt (30%), credit history length (15%), new-credit pursuit (10%), and credit mix (5%) — are used to price and underwrite personal auto and homeowners specifically, and are legally barred from incorporating race, religion, gender, age, income, or location ([NAIC Consumer Insight](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-credit-based-insurance-scores-arent-same-credit-score-understand-how-credit-and-other-factors)). California, Hawaii, and Massachusetts ban their use outright; Maryland and a cluster of other states impose partial restrictions; nine states (including Texas, Florida, and Illinois) specifically bar treating "no credit history" as equivalent to bad credit — and because several bans are explicitly temporary (Washington's, Nevada's) or tied to pending legislation, this is a genuinely moving regulatory patchwork rather than a fixed list ([Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/which-states-prohibit-or-restrict-the-use-of-credit-based-insurance-scores/); [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/resources/details/states-consider-limits-on-insurers-use-of-consumer-credit-info)). No source found evidence that credit-based scoring is material to commercial P&C underwriting — commercial risk selection relies on loss history (E-Mods) and business financials instead.

On **AI/ML-assisted and algorithmic underwriting**, the honest 2025–2026 picture requires separating verified, carrier-disclosed deployments from vendor and consultancy projection. On the verified side: Lemonade closed 2025 with **96% of first-notice-of-loss claims** handled by an AI agent with no human intervention and 55% of all claims resolved fully automatically from intake to payout ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/earnings/2026/lemonade-and-porch-show-ai-is-rewriting-insurance-math/)); Hiscox, partnered with Google Cloud (BigQuery/Vertex AI), cut lead underwriting time on its London Market sabotage-and-terrorism book from **three days to three minutes**, a named, press-released, trade-press-covered deployment ([Insurance Post](https://www.postonline.co.uk/news/7955613/hiscox-uses-ai-to-slash-underwriting-from-three-days-to-three-minutes)). By contrast, McKinsey's claim that AI-augmented underwriting "can improve combined ratios by 5–10 percentage points," and vendor-blog claims of Swiss Re achieving "170% ROI with a 7.3-month payback" or named production deployments at AIG and N2G Worldwide, could not be traced back to a primary carrier disclosure or verifiable trade-press source in this research and should be treated as directional, motivated-source claims rather than confirmed industry facts.

**Straight-through processing (STP) rate** — the share of submissions decided with zero manual touch — is the clearest available KPI for how far this automation has actually progressed, and the benchmarks diverge sharply by line and by definition. Mid-market commercial multi-line business realistically lands around **50–65% STP** with a well-designed rules layer, with some insurers targeting 60–70% as they modernize off legacy cores ([Decerto](https://www.decerto.com/us/post/how-an-underwriting-workbench-streamlines-risk-assessment)); large commercial and specialty lines stay well below that because they are broker-distributed and genuinely heterogeneous. Personal-lines figures conflict outright depending on definition — one source citing Aite-Novarica reports just **7% industry-wide STP** with less than half of insurers using it at all, while another claims "more than three-quarters" of personal-lines transactions process straight through at some carriers — a gap that reflects strict zero-touch measurement versus partial-automation measurement rather than a resolvable single number. A genuinely useful industry insight buried in this ambiguity: practitioners explicitly treat 100% STP as a *bad* goal, because pushing automation to auto-decide risks it should never own autonomously is cited as a direct mechanism by which adverse selection enters a book — STP should be optimized for clean, simple risk only, not chased as an unconditional ceiling.

## Renewal underwriting: continuous re-scoring punctuated by regulatory clocks

Renewal underwriting is architecturally distinct from new-business underwriting, and counterintuitively is often described as *more* manual per-file than new business despite being lower-risk on average — underwriters re-pull data and re-check flags on policies that mostly haven't changed in 12 months ([Majesco](https://www.majesco.com/blog/taming-the-circus-of-commercial-underwriting/)). The realistic operational model is a continuous rules gate rather than a binary choice: an automated re-score runs on every renewal, pulling refreshed MVR, CLUE/claims-history, credit-score, and exposure data and comparing it against *current* appetite and rating rules (which may themselves have shifted since the policy was last underwritten); accounts that clear every check auto-renew or renew with minimal rate/form updates, while any tripped rule — a referral reason, an authority-limit breach, a flagged exposure change — routes the file to something closer to a full new-business review: fresh risk assessment, possibly a new inspection, and a re-decided eligibility and pricing outcome rather than a confirmatory one ([practoinsura.com](https://practoinsura.com/blog/insurance-underwriting-automation-guide/); [Insurity Knowledge Center](https://knowledgecenter.insurity.com/BridgeSpecialty/2.4.87/Doc/Documents/renewalunderwritingrequired.htm)). Full re-underwriting triggers cluster at two levels: policy-level (adverse claims activity, a material exposure change, a tripped referral threshold) and book-level — insurers set target loss ratios per line at the start of each year, and a line that misses its target prompts a cross-functional corrective-action review that can revise guidelines or non-renew entire segments, which then executes as a wave of individual policy-level non-renewals timed to each renewal date rather than one simultaneous action ([Tower Group 10-K via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001289592/000095012308002992/y50172e10vk.htm)).

The personal-vs-commercial timeline gap here is stark. Commercial renewal underwriting is a long, broker-mediated process beginning **90–120 days before expiration** with defined checkpoints — at 120 days identify the renewal and prepare the file, at 90 days collect updated exposure data and set market strategy, at 60 days submit to markets, at 30 days present options and bind — because the process routinely involves re-marketing to multiple carriers ([OpsRev](https://www.opsrev.ai/blog/commercial-insurance-renewal-checklist/)). Personal-lines renewal is, by contrast, a largely automated, near-real-time batch re-score triggered off the expiration date within an agency management system, with no comparable human-managed calendar published by carriers ([getstrada.com](https://www.getstrada.com/blog/renewal-automation)).

Non-renewal communication sits inside a genuinely fragmented, state-by-state notice regime that any multi-state carrier has to calendar carefully. New York requires 45–60 days' notice stating the specific reason; California requires 45 days generally but **75 days for residential policies** (with the existing policy automatically extending if the carrier misses that window); Florida requires 120 days for homeowners/residential property but only 45 days for most other P&C lines, plus a 90-day moratorium after a hurricane; Texas and Minnesota both require 60 days ([NY DFS](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/help_for_homeowners/insurance/cancellations_and_nonrenewals); [Cal. Ins. Code §678](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/insurance-code/ins-sect-678/); [Florida Statute §627.4133](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0600-0699%2F0627%2FSections%2F0627.4133.html); [Minn. Stat. §65A.29](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/65a.29)). Because most states require the notice to state the *specific* reason, carriers' internal underwriting files must capture a defensible reason code at the moment the decision is made, not reconstructed afterward — directly reinforcing the documentation discipline described earlier.

There is a sharp operational and regulatory difference between an individual for-cause non-renewal and a **class- or geography-wide appetite withdrawal**. The former is executed quietly, policy by policy, under the standard notice rules above. The latter is a regulator-gated, public process: most states require an insurer to file a formal withdrawal plan — stating the reason, scope, policy/exposure counts, and notice templates — with the commissioner roughly **one year before completion**, and policyholder/agent notifications cannot go out until the plan is approved ([S.C. Code §38-61-80](https://law.justia.com/codes/south-carolina/title-38/chapter-61/section-38-61-80/)). The real-world 2023–2025 California and Florida pullbacks show this mechanism operating at genuinely massive scale: State Farm paused new California homeowners business in May 2023 and then announced non-renewal of roughly 30,000 homeowners and 42,000 landlord policies in March 2024 — about 2% of its entire California book — citing inflation, catastrophe exposure, reinsurance cost, and regulatory limitations; Allstate, Farmers, USAA, and over a dozen smaller carriers followed similar patterns, while California's Insurance Commissioner has repeatedly imposed mandatory one-year non-renewal moratoriums covering more than a million policies in declared wildfire disaster areas as a regulatory counterweight ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/state-farm-california-homeowners-coverage-wont-renew-72000-homes-apartments/); [California DOI](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0400-news/0100-press-releases/2025/release062-2025.cfm)). Florida's statewide non-renewal rate reached **3.35% of homeowners policies in 2024 — the highest in the country**, roughly 1.7 times its 2018 rate ([FLOIR](https://floir.gov/docs-sf/property-casualty-libraries/property-insurance-market-overview/insurance-update-may-2024.pdf?sfvrsn=1f0259bb_2)).

## Profitability, the underwriting cycle, and how reinsurance pricing reaches into appetite

The three standard underwriting-profitability metrics measure genuinely different things. **Loss ratio** (incurred losses plus loss-adjustment expense, divided by earned premium) isolates risk-selection and claims quality; **expense ratio** (underwriting expenses over written premium) isolates operating and distribution efficiency; **combined ratio**, their sum, nets both into a single verdict on whether the core insurance business — before any investment income — made or lost money, with a result under 100% denoting an underwriting profit ([IRMI](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/combined-ratio)). A combined ratio above 100% does not automatically mean the insurer lost money overall, since investment income earned on "float" can still produce total profitability — but a persistently high combined ratio signals structural dependence on market-driven investment returns rather than underwriting discipline ([Wikipedia: Underwriting profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriting_profit)).

By these metrics, 2025 was the US P&C industry's best underwriting year in over a decade: the combined ratio improved **3.7 points to roughly 92.2–92.9**, net underwriting income nearly tripled to $60.9 billion, and catastrophe losses contributed only 7.6 points to the combined ratio versus 8.8 in 2024 ([AM Best via Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/02/24/859393.htm)). But that aggregate figure masks real divergence by line: 2025 personal auto's net combined ratio was 91.8, homeowners' was 88.1 (its lowest in over a decade), workers' comp ran about 91 — while **general liability's combined ratio was forecast at 107.1, an outright underwriting loss**, driven by social inflation ([Insurance Business Magazine/Triple-I](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/property/property-casualty-combined-ratio-hits-decade-low-as-2025-closes-strong-575604.aspx)). Nuclear verdicts (jury awards over $10 million) rose from 27 in 2023 to **135 in 2024 — a 52% increase** — with 2024 awards exceeding $31 billion, and social inflation in claim costs reached a 20-year high of roughly 7% in 2023, now exceeding economic inflation as the primary driver of casualty claims cost ([Insurance Journal/Claims Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2024/10/07/795733.htm)). This is the clearest evidence that the **underwriting cycle is currently split by line**: property and property-catastrophe business is softening sharply, while casualty/liability remains structurally hard-to-loss-making for reasons reinsurance capacity cannot fix.

The underwriting cycle itself — the recurring oscillation between soft markets (abundant capacity, falling rates, loose terms) and hard markets (constrained capacity, rising rates, strict terms) — is typically triggered into its hard phase by a shock: major catastrophe losses, reserve deterioration, or a reinsurance capacity shortfall ([Wikipedia: Insurance cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance_cycle)). The 2023 hard-market peak was driven by a genuine reinsurance supply crunch: US property catastrophe reinsurance rates rose **45–100% at the January 2023 renewal**, the sharpest increase since 1992, driven by Hurricane Ian and a global $25–50 billion catastrophe-capacity supply-demand shortfall ([MyNewMarkets](https://www.mynewmarkets.com/articles/184094/january-renewals-see-hardest-property-catastrophe-reinsurance-rates-in-generation)). The hard-market mechanics at the primary level were blunt and portfolio-wide rather than fine-grained per-risk judgment: named carriers (State Farm, Allstate, Kemper subsidiaries) stopped accepting new homeowners applications in entire states, citing construction-cost inflation, catastrophe exposure, and reinsurance cost together ([Yahoo Finance/Kiplinger synthesis](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/4-more-insurers-leaving-california-161957340.html)).

The reinsurance-to-primary feedback mechanism works through **attachment points and retention**. When a reinsurer raises price, raises the attachment point, or shrinks capacity at treaty renewal, the primary carrier's own retained layer — the risk it keeps net of reinsurance — grows larger relative to its capital base for the same book of business; concrete 2023 evidence of this showed "a far greater proportion of claims... being retained on the insurer's balance sheet, rather than being ceded to reinsurers," reversing a pattern cedents had grown accustomed to during the preceding soft market ([Artemis.bm citing Jefferies](https://www.artemis.bm/news/higher-reinsurance-attachment-points-the-real-hard-market-story-jefferies/)). That larger retained exposure consumes more of the carrier's capital and volatility budget per policy, which is translated downstream into updated underwriting-authority instructions: lower per-risk and per-territory limits, higher required catastrophe deductibles, tighter eligibility rules, and in extreme cases a formal "closed to new business" instruction for a state or peril — field underwriters receive these as revised guidelines from head office rather than exercising independent judgment. The mechanism runs symmetrically in reverse: Travelers' own CFO stated the company bought *more* casualty reinsurance protection at a *lower* attachment point at a January 1 renewal, explicitly illustrating how cheaper, more available reinsurance frees up primary underwriting capacity ([Reinsurance News](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/travelers-increased-casualty-reinsurance-coverage-at-lower-attachment-point-at-jan-1-cfo-frey/)). This reverse direction is exactly what has been playing out through 2025–2026: Howden Re estimated risk-adjusted property catastrophe reinsurance pricing fell **14.7% year-on-year at the January 2026 renewal** — the largest decline since 2014 — with global property catastrophe rates down a further 16% at the July 2026 mid-year renewal, as record reinsurance capital (including catastrophe bonds) outpaced demand following a quieter 2025 hurricane season ([Artemis.bm](https://www.artemis.bm/news/property-cat-reinsurance-down-14-7-retrocession-down-16-5-at-jan-2026-renewals-howden-re/); [Artemis.bm, July 2026](https://www.artemis.bm/news/global-and-us-property-cat-rates-down-16-apac-19-after-july-renewals-in-2026-guy-carpenter/)). Analysts caution this softening is not a full return to pre-2023 pricing, and that attachment points are "largely holding" even as rate-on-line falls — reinsurers appear to be defending the structural risk-transfer discipline won in 2023 while competing on price ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2026/09/08/884291.htm)).

## Where the machine still breaks: consistency, speed, fraud, and new perils

Six well-documented pain points recur across the industry, each with a distinct, verifiable response pattern. **Inconsistent guideline application** persists because appetite still mostly lives in unstructured documents; a diagnostic cited in industry commentary asks three underwriters how they'd apply one specific rule (e.g., "roof age over 15 years") and finds three different answers — meaning "the rule does not exist as an enforced rule; it exists as a suggestion" ([Decerto](https://www.decerto.com/us/post/top-features-to-look-for-in-an-underwriting-workbench-solution)). The external regulatory check on this — state market conduct exams — is itself acknowledged by the US Government Accountability Office to be structurally uneven, since "no generally accepted standards exist for market conduct regulation" and states apply the NAIC's model handbook inconsistently, producing "spotty coverage from state to state" ([GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-03-433-highlights.pdf)).

**Commercial-lines turnaround** is slow mainly because of submission intake and data-assembly friction rather than underwriting judgment itself — underwriters reportedly spend 30–60 minutes per submission on clerical extraction before evaluating risk, because ACORD forms, supplemental applications, loss runs, and narrative emails "rarely agree perfectly" and require manual reconciliation ([Pibit.ai](https://pibit.ai/blog/submission-turnaround-time-commercial-pc)). A genuinely physical bottleneck compounds this: new commercial property loss-control surveys typically take 30–60 days to schedule and complete and can leave underwriting terms provisional until the report is reviewed — a constraint software cannot compress below the physics of scheduling an on-site visit ([WSRB](https://www1.wsrb.com/blog/property-inspections-reduce-loss); [SBN Software](https://sbnsoftware.com/blog/what-is-a-loss-control-survey-and-how-does-it-differ-from-an-inspection/)).

**Catastrophe aggregation management** has moved from periodic (quarterly/annual) portfolio roll-ups toward real-time, API-level checks at the point of bind — platforms like Moody's RMS ExposureIQ and smaller MGA-focused tools (Opterrix, BirdsEyeView) are marketed to flag or block a bind that would push accumulated exposure in a geographic peril zone over a pre-set limit, rather than revealing over-concentration only after the fact ([Moody's ExposureIQ](https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/who-we-serve/insurance/intelligent-risk-platform/exposureiq.html)). **Application-stage fraud and misrepresentation** is addressed less through a specific detection technology than through a legal and organizational structure: the NAIC's model framework requires a full-time **Special Investigation Unit (SIU)** kept organizationally separate from underwriting and claims, and a companion model law carves out an explicit exception letting insurers withhold their specific reasoning when they suspect fraud or material misrepresentation — protecting their ability to act without tipping off exactly what was detected ([InsuranceFraud.org](https://insurancefraud.org/requirement_types/siu-requirement/); [NAIC Model Law 670](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/model-law-670.pdf)). **Legacy policy administration system friction** is corroborated independently across multiple vendor sources describing the same underlying pattern in different carrier environments: data for claims, policy, billing, and underwriting live in separate silos, forcing "swivel-chair" rekeying of the same submission into multiple systems — a genuine, widespread issue, though what modernization fixes is specifically the mechanical data-flow problem, not the underlying need for physical inspections or judgment calls on ambiguous risk ([OIP Insurtech](https://www.oipinsurtech.com/data-silos-in-insurance/); [InsOps](https://insops.com/blogs/data-silos-in-p-c-insurance-why-your-claims-policy-and-billing-data-live-in-different-systems)).

Two emerging practices are reshaping underwriting for perils where traditional indemnity coverage performs poorly. **Parametric insurance** pays a pre-agreed amount when an objectively measured, third-party-verified parameter — wind speed, earthquake ground acceleration, storm-surge water height — crosses a threshold, rather than paying based on an adjuster's assessment of actual loss, which eliminates the claims-adjustment lag entirely ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12670/IN12670.3.pdf)). It is genuinely live today: CCRIF issued an estimated $85 million in parametric payouts to five Caribbean governments within two weeks of Hurricane Beryl in 2024; Hannover Re's Acorn Re catastrophe bond series (the 2026 iteration targets $200 million) covers US earthquake risk with a sliding-scale payout tied to magnitude and location, concentrated 72% in California; and Aon, Floodbase, and Swiss Re Corporate Solutions launched a parametric storm-surge product in February 2025 specifically because storm surge can account for more than a third of total hurricane loss cost yet is frequently excluded or subject to high deductibles in traditional property policies ([Artemis.bm](https://www.artemis.bm/news/hannover-re-targets-200m-seventh-acorn-re-parametric-us-earthquake-cat-bond/); [Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2025/02/05/810832.htm)). Parametric underwriting shifts carrier effort away from post-event loss adjustment and toward pre-bind model/data-quality work — ensuring the trigger index genuinely correlates with real economic loss to minimize basis risk — a fundamentally different skill set from inspection-and-valuation-based property underwriting.

**Telematics-based usage underwriting** replaces static, point-in-time rating factors with continuous behavioral data — speed, braking, mileage, time of day — collected via a plug-in device or app, with Progressive's Snapshot program as the operational leader: the carrier explicitly recalculates a personalized rate at renewal based on accumulated driving data rather than fixing it at policy inception ([Progressive](https://www.progressive.com/support/legal/snapshot-app-terms-conditions/)). The NAIC frames this as a structural shift from "historical factors: driving records, credit scores, vehicle type, and location" toward "individual and current driving behaviors instead of relying on statistics based on past trends" ([NAIC](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-understanding-usage-based-insurance)). Adoption figures vary widely by source and vintage — a 2025 report cites 14.4% of personal-lines motor policies now including telematics — but the more reliable finding is directional convergence: nine of the top ten US auto insurers already had telematics programs as early as 2019, and commercial fleet telematics has pushed further toward genuine continuous underwriting, with SambaSafety reporting 88% of fleets using telematics for safety purposes and mid-term premium adjustments triggered by detected shifts in driving behavior ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/06/13/3099019/0/en/Usage-Based-and-Telematics-Motor-Insurance-Report-2025-Telematics-Becomes-a-Consumer-Favorite-as-14-4-of-Policies-Now-Include-It.html); [TruckerCloud](https://truckercloud.com/post/driving-efficiency-and-accuracy-the-power-of-telematics-in-commercial-fleet-underwriting)).

## Conclusion

The thread connecting every section above is that underwriting discipline is enforced far more by *structure* than by *judgment calls* — referral thresholds, authority grids, rules engines, and regulator-mandated documentation do the consistency work that individual underwriter wisdom alone cannot reliably deliver across thousands of files and dozens of jurisdictions. That structural emphasis also explains why the industry's current AI wave is landing unevenly: the verified deployments (Lemonade's claims automation, Hiscox's lead-underwriting tool) succeed precisely where they slot into existing rules-and-referral architecture rather than replacing underwriter judgment outright, while the much larger claimed productivity gains from vendors and consultancies remain unverifiable against primary disclosure. The most consequential near-term dynamic to watch is the widening split between property/catastrophe underwriting — now genuinely softening as reinsurance capital floods back in — and casualty/liability underwriting, where social inflation has made the hard market self-sustaining in a way reinsurance pricing alone cannot reverse; a carrier's or MGA's appetite posture over the next renewal cycle will likely diverge sharply by line rather than move as one undifferentiated market, and the residual-market mechanisms (FAIR plans, Citizens, TWIA) will keep absorbing whatever property risk private appetite continues to shed in catastrophe-exposed geographies regardless of how that broader cycle turns.
