# Mapping the machinery of P&C insurance

Property & Casualty insurance looks, from the outside, like a pile of jargon — binders, endorsements, loss costs, cedents — but underneath it is a surprisingly coherent system built around one problem: pricing and distributing risk transfer at scale across 50 separate legal jurisdictions. The core structural fact a newcomer needs is that **personal lines are standardized and algorithmic while commercial lines are judgment-driven and customized** ([insnerds.com](https://insnerds.com/5-differences-commercial-personal-lines-insurance/)), and nearly everything else in the industry — how policies move through their lifecycle, how underwriting authority is delegated, how premiums are built, how claims get paid, how carriers offload their own risk through reinsurance, and how regulation is organized — follows from that one divide and from the fact that the US regulates insurance **state by state**, not federally, under the 1945 McCarran-Ferguson Act ([Wikipedia: McCarran-Ferguson Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarran%E2%80%93Ferguson_Act)). A policy's life runs through a predictable sequence — quote, bind, issue, endorsement, renewal or non-renewal, cancellation, reinstatement, rewrite — each with a distinct contractual meaning that a policy administration system (PAS) must model as a distinct transaction type, not a status flag. Underwriting authority cascades from carrier to MGA/MGU to retail agent in tiers bounded by binding-authority agreements, with referral thresholds acting as capital controls rather than courtesy. Premiums are built multiplicatively from a base rate times rating-variable relativities, informed by advisory loss costs from ISO and filed with state regulators under a legal test — not excessive, not inadequate, not unfairly discriminatory — that the Casualty Actuarial Society operationalizes as "actuarially sound." Claims run FNOL through investigation, reserving, settlement, and subrogation, feeding loss history straight back into underwriting. And reinsurance — "insurance for insurance companies" ([NAIC CIPR: Reinsurance](https://content.naic.org/cipr_topics/topic_reinsurance.htm)) — lets primary carriers cede risk via treaty or facultative, proportional or non-proportional structures, entirely outside the policyholder's view. Understanding these pieces as one interlocking system, rather than memorizing vocabulary in isolation, is what separates fluency from a glossary lookup.

## Personal and commercial lines split on standardization, not just market segment

The most useful first distinction in P&C is not "who buys the policy" but **how much human judgment the pricing and underwriting require**. Personal lines (auto, homeowners) insure individuals and households against a narrow, well-understood set of exposures — essentially a vehicle or a dwelling plus the people around it — and can be rated almost entirely by algorithm off a small set of variables; commercial lines insure businesses whose operations, revenue, payroll, and liability exposures vary so widely that underwriters must interpret financial statements, loss runs, and operational narratives by hand ([insnerds.com](https://insnerds.com/5-differences-commercial-personal-lines-insurance/)). The clearest technical marker of this divide is liability-exposure scope: commercial underwriters evaluate **four** distinct liability exposures — premises, operations, products, and completed operations — while a personal-lines consumer typically carries only a premises exposure ([insnerds.com](https://insnerds.com/5-differences-commercial-personal-lines-insurance/)). Property coverage scales the same way: personal lines wraps around a home and its contents, while commercial property spans offices, warehouses, and storefronts, with "contents" extending to inventory, machinery, and third-party property ([insnerds.com](https://insnerds.com/5-differences-commercial-personal-lines-insurance/)).

Within personal lines, the standard homeowners form (**HO-3**) illustrates a hybrid coverage structure every engineer modeling coverage logic should internalize: it is **open-peril** (all-risk) for the dwelling itself — meaning the insurer must prove an exclusion applies to deny a claim — but **named-peril** for personal property, covering only perils explicitly listed, which flips the burden of proof onto the insured ([The Zebra: HO-3](https://www.thezebra.com/homeowners-insurance/policies/what-is-ho-3-insurance-policy/); [Coverage Classroom: Named Peril vs Open Peril](https://coverageclassroom.com/learn/named-peril-vs-open-peril)). Commercial lines, meanwhile, fragment into structurally distinct sub-lines, each anchored to a different **primary rating base**: workers' compensation rates almost entirely off payroll and classification code, refined by an **experience modifier** ("mod") that compares an employer's actual three-year loss history to the expected losses for its class — a mod of 1.0 is average, below 1.0 rewards good loss control, above 1.0 penalizes poor history ([NCCI: ABCs of Experience Rating](https://www.ncci.com/articles/documents/uw_abc_exp_rating.pdf)). Commercial general liability (CGL) is written on either an **occurrence** basis (covers injury/damage happening during the policy period regardless of when the claim surfaces later) or a **claims-made** basis (covers claims first made during the policy period, tied to a retroactive date, and generally cheaper because it caps the insurer's tail exposure) ([InsureTutor: CGL Occurrence vs Claims-Made](https://insuretutor.com/articles/commercial-general-liability-occurrence-vs-claims-made-claims-adjuster)). Commercial auto carries higher liability limits and broader vehicle/driver coverage than personal auto, which explicitly excludes business use — a gap commonly plugged by separate **hired and non-owned auto (HNOA)** coverage for vehicles a business uses but doesn't own ([Blake Insurance Group: Commercial Auto vs HNOA](https://blakeinsurancegroup.com/commercial-auto-vs-hired-non-owned-auto-insurance/)). Cyber insurance, a newer line, splits into first-party coverage (the insured's own breach-response costs) and third-party liability (harm the insured causes others), structurally distinct from professional liability/E&O, which covers only third-party claims over failed service performance and typically excludes breach-related costs entirely ([At-Bay: Tech E&O vs Cyber](https://www.at-bay.com/articles/technology-errors-and-omissions-vs-cyber-insurance/)). And commercial property itself bifurcates by packaging flexibility: a **Business Owners Policy (BOP)** bundles GL, property, and business-interruption coverage into a fixed package for simple small-business risk, while a **Commercial Package Policy (CPP)** lets larger or more complex accounts pick and choose coverage parts under one policy number ([Insureon: BOP vs CPP](https://www.insureon.com/small-business-insurance/compare/commercial-package-vs-business-owners-policy)).

## The policy lifecycle runs on an escalating ladder of commitment

Every P&C policy moves through a sequence of named transactions, each carrying a precise contractual meaning that any PAS data model needs to represent as a distinct transaction type. A **quote** is explicitly non-binding — an estimate of premium for the coverage requested, with no confirmation of coverage ([Insurance Training Center: Binders Explained](https://insurancetrainingcenter.com/resource/insurance-binders-explained/)). To **bind** is to confirm coverage is actually in force, even before the formal policy document exists, typically via a short-lived **binder** (30–90 days) that functions as a temporary policy ([At-Bay: What is bind insurance?](https://www.at-bay.com/faqs/what-is-bind-insurance/)). **Issuance** is the durable, fully-documented policy that eventually replaces the binder once underwriting and paperwork are finalized. This quote → bind → issue sequence is an escalating commitment ladder: no obligation, then immediate enforceable coverage, then full documentation — a structure that lets coverage start (say, at a real-estate closing) before the paperwork catches up.

Once in force, a policy changes through an **endorsement** — a form that adds to, removes, or modifies the base policy's provisions, used for in-term changes like adding an insured, a location, or broadening coverage ([IRMI: Endorsement](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/endorsement)). Endorsements are priced **pro rata**: an additional premium (AP) or return premium (RP) is calculated only against the days remaining in the current term, not a fresh full-term premium — so "effective date of the endorsement" and "days remaining in term" are load-bearing fields for any premium-calculation engine ([Merit Insurance: Prorated Billing](https://merit-insurance.com/prorated-billing-amend-your-insurance-mid-term/)). Notably, deductibles generally cannot be *reduced* mid-term (absent a mortgagee requirement) even though coverage and limits can be *increased* at any time — an asymmetry that blocks an insured from cheaply lowering their deductible right before an anticipated claim.

Termination splits into two legally distinct paths with very different regulatory latitude. **Cancellation** ends a policy *before* its natural expiration and, once a policy has been in force more than 60 days, is heavily restricted by state law to specific causes — nonpayment, fraud or material misrepresentation, or increased hazard — with notice periods commonly running 10–60 days depending on the state ([IRMI: Notice of Cancellation Clauses](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/notice-of-cancellation-clauses); [NY DFS: Cancellations and Nonrenewals](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/help_for_homeowners/insurance/cancellations_and_nonrenewals)). **Non-renewal**, by contrast, simply lets the policy expire at the end of its term without a new one starting, and insurers face far fewer restrictions on *why* they can do it — which is precisely why non-renewal, not cancellation, is the primary lever insurers use to exit unwanted geography or risk classes at scale, such as wildfire- or hurricane-exposed homeowners books, while cancellation stays reserved for acute, cause-based terminations discovered mid-term. New York's homeowners rules illustrate the asymmetry concretely: cancellation after the first 60 days is prohibited for three years except for a short enumerated list of causes, while non-renewal requires only 45–60 days' notice with no restriction on the underlying reason ([NY DFS](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/help_for_homeowners/insurance/cancellations_and_nonrenewals)).

Two further transactions resolve the ambiguity between "fixing" a policy and "replacing" it. **Reinstatement** reverses a cancellation with zero coverage gap — the reinstatement date is set equal to the original cancellation date, as if the cancellation never happened ([Guidewire: Policy Rewrite Transactions](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/bc/202411/cloudapibf/app/bc/060_policyTrans/05_rewrites/c_rewrites.html)). A **rewrite**, by contrast, cancels the original policy outright and issues a brand-new one — used when a gap in coverage is unavoidable, when a submission error needs correcting to match the policyholder's original intent, or when the policy needs to move to a different insured account entirely (the cited example: transferring a child's auto policy off a parent's household account) ([Guidewire](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/bc/202411/cloudapibf/app/bc/060_policyTrans/05_rewrites/c_rewrites.html)). The practical modeling implication is that a rewrite should appear as two linked policy records (old terminated, new created with a back-reference), while reinstatement is a status change on the *same* record — and renewal, the final lifecycle stage, is lighter still: insurers begin renewal underwriting 60–120 days before expiration (commercial lines further out than personal lines) as a re-check of loss history and exposure change rather than a full new-business underwrite, generally preserving the same policy number into a new term ([OIP InsurTech: 2025 Guide to Renewal Underwriting](https://www.oipinsurtech.com/2025-guide-to-renewal-underwriting/)).

## Underwriting authority cascades from carrier to MGA to agent through referral gates

Underwriting is the process of deciding whether to accept a risk, at what price, and under what conditions — sorting applicants into **standard/preferred** ("clean"), **substandard** (insurable but at a higher price or with restrictions), or **decline** (uninsurable at any price the insurer would offer) ([InsureTutor: Risk Classifications](https://insuretutor.com/articles/risk-classifications-preferred-standard-substandard)). The stated goal is equity among risk classes while protecting insurer solvency — accepting too many bad risks threatens insolvency ([Achievable: Underwriting](https://app.achievable.me/study/insurance-property/learn/underwriting)), which is why underwriting guidelines function as the practical, risk-level enforcement of the actuarial assumptions baked into the rates.

Who actually gets to make that accept/price/bind decision — and without checking back with anyone else — is governed by **binding authority**, a power an insurer grants to commit it to a risk without prior transaction-level approval ([Square One: Binding Authority](https://www.squareone.ca/resource-centres/insurance-glossary/binding-authority)). That authority runs in tiers. Retail agents and brokers generally hold none of it and must submit each risk to the carrier for an underwriting decision; **Managing General Agents (MGAs)** and **Managing General Underwriters (MGUs)** hold broad, contractually delegated binding authority and can quote, underwrite, and bind immediately within agreed limits, effectively operating as an outsourced underwriting department ([RMS Programs: MGA vs Standard Carrier](https://www.rmsprograms.com/blog/mga-vs-standard-carrier-for-retail-agents/)); and the carrier's own underwriters sit at the top, retaining ultimate balance-sheet and regulatory responsibility no matter how far authority is delegated downstream ([Kasko: MGA vs Carrier](https://www.kasko.io/post/managing-general-agent-vs-carrier)). This delegation is never unlimited — the binding authority agreement specifies maximum per-risk and aggregate limits, permitted classes and territories, excluded perils, and the **referral triggers** that pull a decision back to a higher-authority underwriter whenever a submission falls outside the pre-agreed parameters ([Novatae: What is an MGA?](https://novatae.com/news/what-is-an-mga-in-insurance)). Functionally, referral thresholds work like exception handling in software: the normal path is handled automatically within the delegate's authority, while anything unusual escalates — and because authority is tiered *within* carriers too (junior underwriter → senior → chief), a referral threshold is best understood as a **capital control**, not a comment on any individual's competence ([domainexperts.dev](https://domainexperts.dev/roles/insurance-underwriter/)). The US MGA channel is sizable and growing: premium flowing through MGAs rose **16% to $114.1 billion in 2024**, per Conning's 2025 study, with fronting carriers backing more than $18 billion of that volume ([Vitesse: What Is an MGA](https://www.vitesse.io/insights/what-is-a-managing-general-agent)). Because carriers retain balance-sheet and regulatory responsibility even while delegating broadly, they compensate for reduced transaction-level control with after-the-fact oversight — audit rights and periodic **bordereaux** reporting of risks written and claims paid — rather than approving every bound policy individually ([Sarvada: Delegated Authority, Binders and Bordereaux](https://sarvada.ai/underwriting-risk/delegated-authority-binder-bordereaux-underwriting-governance-india-2026)).

## Premiums are built multiplicatively from base rates and filed loss costs

Conceptually, a P&C premium is constructed by starting from a **base rate** — the insurer's expected cost for a line at reference limits and deductibles, plus a required profit margin ([Henssler Financial](https://www.henssler.com/auto-insurance-premiums/)) — and multiplying it by a series of **rating factors**, or relativities, tied to rating variables such as territory, vehicle type, construction class, or claims history. Each applicant is sorted into a rating class or tier for every variable, and the product of all the relativities times the base rate yields the premium: base rate × relativity₁ × relativity₂ × … × relativityₙ ([Salesforce Trailhead: Insurance Rating Calculation](https://trailhead.salesforce.com/content/learn/modules/insurance-quoting-foundations/calculate-quotes-with-rating); [Loss Data Analytics, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.06718)). This multiplicative structure is deliberately modular — an insurer can add or recalibrate a single rating variable (say, a new credit-based insurance score) without rebuilding the entire rate structure, since each relativity is estimated largely independently, typically today via generalized linear models.

Much of the underlying loss-cost data behind those relativities is not produced in-house. **ISO** (Insurance Services Office, now a Verisk subsidiary) is an advisory and rating organization, formed in 1971, that develops and publishes **advisory loss costs** — the pure projected cost of claims and allocated adjustment expenses, excluding overhead and profit — by classification, across a statistical database spanning more than **5.5 billion records across 17 lines of insurance** ([Wikipedia: ISO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance_Services_Office)). To turn a loss cost into an actual rate, each insurer applies its own **loss cost multiplier** covering its expenses and profit — and in most states that multiplier itself must be filed with and approved by the insurance department ([ISO Commercial Property Rating, via search synthesis](http://www.rnc-advantageplus.com/rnc-advantageplus/pfm/100/130_0502.HTM)). Carriers are free to deviate from ISO content entirely, filing proprietary rates to differentiate in the market or calibrate their own risk appetite ([ValueMomentum: ISO Strategy](https://blog.valuemomentum.com/long-term-what-is-my-iso-strategy-outsource-to-vendor-do-i-want-to-stay-current-with-iso)) — meaning ISO's loss costs are an *input* that still passes through the same regulatory review as a fully independent filing, not a binding national rate.

That regulatory review turns on a near-universal legal standard: a rate must be **not excessive, not inadequate, and not unfairly discriminatory**. The Casualty Actuarial Society's Statement of Principles operationalizes this as "actuarially sound" — a rate is reasonable if it is an actuarially sound estimate of the expected value of all future costs associated with an individual risk transfer ([CAS: Statement of Principles Regarding P&C Ratemaking](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2021-06/Statement%20of%20Principles%20Regarding%20P%26C%20Casualty%20Insurance%20Ratemaking_2021.pdf)). Critically, "unfairly discriminatory" is not a ban on risk-based price variation — it requires only that price differences be *equitably tied* to differences in expected cost, which is the regulatory foundation that legitimizes rating tiers in the first place, as long as they're empirically grounded rather than arbitrary. States enforce this standard through a **rate filing**, submitted mostly via the NAIC's electronic SERFF system, under one of several review regimes: **prior approval** (filing must be approved before use, sometimes with automatic "deemer" approval after a fixed number of days), **file-and-use**, **use-and-file**, or **flex rating** (a tolerance band within which changes need no prior approval) ([Insurance.ca.gov: Prior Approval Rate Filing Instructions](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0250-insurers/0800-rate-filings/0200-prior-approval-factors/upload/PriorAppRateFilingInstructions.pdf)).

## Distribution economics hinge on who owns the customer relationship

P&C products reach customers through several structurally distinct channels, and the legal and economic lines between them do not run in parallel. **Captive agents** work for a single insurer; **independent agents** sell multiple carriers' products; both are legally agents *of the insurer*, owing fiduciary duty to the company that appointed them, not the customer ([Hourly: Captive vs. Independent Agent](https://www.hourly.io/post/captive-agent-vs-independent-agent)). **Brokers**, by contrast, are legally agents of the *buyer* — yet they are still paid by the insurer, via commission on premium, which is the structural source of most broker conflict-of-interest regulation, since the party a broker nominally serves is not the party that pays them ([Umbrex: Insurance Brokers and MGAs](https://umbrex.com/resources/industry-lingo/financial-services-lingo/insurance-brokers-and-mgas-lingo/); [BrokerageAudit: Fiduciary Duty](https://brokerageaudit.com/blog/understanding-fiduciary-duty-insurance-broker-for-insurance-brokers)). Compensation reflects overhead trade-offs: independent agents typically out-earn captive agents by **20–30%** at equivalent production because they absorb their own overhead and own their book of business as a sellable asset, while captive agents trade that upside for carrier-subsidized leads, salary support, and training — often the better entry point for new agents ([Hourly: Captive vs. Independent Agent](https://www.hourly.io/post/captive-agent-vs-independent-agent)).

**MGAs/MGUs** layer underwriting functions on top of distribution and are compensated accordingly: a typical structure combines a retail commission (10–15% of premium) with an underwriting override (5–15%), pushing total MGA compensation to roughly **15–25% of premium** — notably higher than standard broker compensation — and the TMPAA's 2025 Program Business Economics Report puts average total specialty-MGA compensation at **24.3% of written premium** ([Insurnest: Pet Insurance Commission Rates](https://insurnest.com/blog/pet-insurance-commission-rates-mga-carriers/); [Insurnest: Agent Commission Incentive Programs](https://insurnest.com/blog/agent-commission-incentive-programs-pet-insurance-mga/)). Profit-sharing schedules are often layered on top to align MGA incentives with underwriting *quality*, not just volume — one cited example pays a 4% bonus if the loss ratio stays under 40%, 2% under 50%, and nothing above 60% ([Insurnest: Commission-Based Revenue Model](https://insurnest.com/blog/commission-based-revenue-model-mga-pet-insurance-zero-upfront-risk/)).

**Direct-to-consumer (DTC)** carriers like GEICO sell straight to the customer with no agent commission at all, which traditional agent commissions (15–20%+ of premium) make into a meaningful structural cost advantage — but the carrier must then own marketing, sales, and the full customer lifetime value itself, rather than renting an intermediary's existing relationship ([QED Investors: The DTC advantage in insurance](https://qedinvestors.substack.com/p/the-direct-to-consumer-advantage)). GEICO and Progressive together hold roughly **28% of the US personal auto market**, yet the comparison between them is instructive: despite GEICO's cost-structure edge, Progressive's loss costs run about 11 points better, attributed to data-driven underwriting tools like telematics-based Snapshot — evidence that **technology-driven risk selection can offset a distribution-cost disadvantage** ([QED Investors](https://qedinvestors.substack.com/p/the-direct-to-consumer-advantage)). The underlying pattern across all these channels is that the economic owner of the customer relationship — agent, broker, MGA, or carrier itself — also tends to own the data loop that drives future underwriting and pricing refinement, which is a large part of why insurtechs chasing DTC distribution have historically invested so heavily in proprietary risk-scoring technology.

## Policy forms layer declarations, insuring agreements, and endorsements like software patches

A P&C policy is assembled from a standard architecture: the **declarations page** ("dec page") lists the named insured, covered property/risks, limits, deductibles, the policy period, and — critically — the full **endorsement schedule** naming every form attached to the policy; the **insuring agreement** is the insurer's broad promise to pay for covered losses; **exclusions** carve back specific perils or situations the insurer won't cover; **conditions** impose duties on both parties (prompt notice of loss, cooperation) that can affect whether a claim gets paid; and **endorsements** are attached documents that add, remove, or modify any of the preceding pieces — with endorsement language controlling whenever it conflicts with the base form ([Sonant: Insurance Endorsement Guide](https://www.sonant.ai/blog/insurance-endorsement)). For an engineer, the useful mental model is a layered-override system: declarations are instance-level configuration applied to a template; the insuring agreement, exclusions, and conditions are the base "class" logic; endorsements are patches evaluated with higher precedence than the base form. Effective coverage is never just the base form — it's the base form as modified by every endorsement listed on that specific policyholder's declarations page.

Where that base form wording comes from varies by a three-tier hierarchy of increasing customization and cost. **ISO forms** are standardized, pre-filed, "court-tested" wording reused across many unaffiliated carriers — cheapest to adopt but offering no competitive differentiation. **Proprietary ("company") forms** are drafted and filed individually by one insurer to broaden coverage for a competitive edge or restrict it for higher-risk business. **Manuscript forms** are fully custom wording negotiated for one specific insured's unique risk, reserved for large or complex accounts where bespoke drafting and state-filing cost is justified by premium size ([FindLaw: Manuscript Policy](https://www.findlaw.com/consumer/insurance/what-is-a-manuscript-policy.html); [Umbrex: Comparing Carriers & Policy Forms](https://umbrex.com/resources/the-busy-consultants-guide-to-business-insurance/comparing-carriers-policy-forms-like-a-pro/)). Because ISO forms are reused verbatim across many carriers and states, judicial interpretation of that identical wording in one coverage dispute becomes persuasive precedent elsewhere — a direct, practical reason standardized language reduces claims litigation risk compared to one-off proprietary or manuscript wording.

Separately from policy *forms*, **ACORD** (Association for Cooperative Operations Research and Development, founded 1970) standardizes the *transactional* layer: a library of paper/PDF forms — the **ACORD 125** commercial application and **ACORD 25** certificate of liability insurance being the most ubiquitous — alongside a much larger library of electronic data standards covering roughly **14,000 data elements for P&C** and more than **1,200 standardized transaction types**, which let agency management systems, carrier policy systems, and rating engines exchange data without bespoke point-to-point integration ([Wikibooks: ACORD](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/XML_-_Managing_Data_Exchange/acord); [Wikipedia: ACORD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACORD)). A point worth flagging for anyone building claims or underwriting software: an **ACORD 25 certificate explicitly does not modify the underlying policy** — it's evidentiary proof that coverage exists at a point in time, requested by a third party like a landlord or general contractor, and treating it as a source of truth for actual coverage terms is a real and common error; the policy plus its declarations and endorsements remains the only actual contract ([useAnvil: ACORD 25](https://www.useanvil.com/forms/insurance/acord-25-certificate-of-insurance/)).

Because insurance is state-regulated, forms generally need state-level filing and, in many states, approval before use — under the same prior-approval / file-and-use / use-and-file spectrum that governs rates. Using an unapproved form is a real regulatory violation exposing the insurer to discipline; notably, at least under New York law, the underlying policy itself still remains valid and enforceable for the policyholder despite the insurer's violation ([NY DFS Opinion No. 05-07-08](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/insurance/ogco2005/rg050708.htm)) — regulators punish the insurer, not the insured, which preserves the consumer-protection purpose of the filing requirement.

## Claims follow a loss through reserving and subrogation back into pricing

A claim's lifecycle begins at **First Notice of Loss (FNOL)** — the policyholder's or third party's first report of an incident, now submitted via phone, agent, app, portal, or increasingly telematics/IoT triggers that detect a loss before anyone calls — capturing the policy number, loss date/time, incident description, and supporting documentation needed to open a claim file ([Sentry: What Is FNOL?](https://www.sentry.com/what-we-offer/resources/articles/what-is-fnol)). From there the claim moves through triage and assignment, **coverage verification** (is the policy in force, does it cover this cause of loss, what limits/deductibles/exclusions apply) paired with an **initial reserve** set early to ensure funds are available, then investigation (interviews, site inspection, documentation), a coverage decision (approve, partially approve, or deny), negotiation and settlement, payment, and — where a third party is at fault — subrogation and recovery ([Decerto: Claims Lifecycle Management](https://www.decerto.com/us/post/claims-lifecycle-management-best-practices-for-insurers)). This whole process operates under the NAIC's model **Unfair Claims Settlement Practices Act (UCSPA)**, which prohibits insurers from misrepresenting policy provisions, failing to promptly acknowledge or investigate claims, refusing to pay without reasonable investigation, or forcing insureds to litigate for amounts clearly owed — turning the "stages" into a compliance timeline that varies by state, not just an internal workflow ([PropertyCasualty360: UCSPA Overview](https://www.propertycasualty360.com/fcs/2025/10/27/unfair-claim-settlement-practices-act--an-overview/)).

A claim is also either **first-party** (filed by the policyholder against their own insurer) or **third-party** (filed by someone else against the at-fault party's insurer); first-party claims move faster and carry direct "bad faith" duties, while third-party claimants have no such direct duty owed to them by the other side's carrier ([Sentry: First-Party vs. Third-Party Claims](https://www.sentry.com/what-we-offer/resources/articles/first-and-third-party-claims)). Handling the claim falls to an **adjuster** — a **staff adjuster** (insurer employee), an **independent adjuster** (contractor hired by the insurer, often for catastrophe overflow volume), or a **public adjuster** (hired by and representing the policyholder, typically on contingency) — and the key difference across all three is simply *loyalty*: staff and independent adjusters work for the insurer's financial interest, public adjusters work for the policyholder's ([AdjusterPro: Types of Insurance Adjusters](https://adjusterpro.com/types-of-insurance-adjusters/)).

**Reserves** are the accounting mechanism that keeps an insurer's balance sheet honest about what it actually owes. A **case reserve** is an adjuster's estimate on a known, already-reported claim; **IBNR (incurred but not reported)** reserves cover losses that have already happened economically but haven't yet surfaced as a filed claim, estimated through actuarial development methods like chain-ladder or Bornhuetter-Ferguson; the sum of reported-claim reserves and IBNR produces the insurer's estimate of **ultimate losses** ([Wikipedia: Incurred but not reported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incurred_but_not_reported); [IRMI: Loss Reserve](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/loss-reserve)). Reserve accuracy matters beyond bookkeeping: it feeds directly into the **loss ratio** — incurred losses (paid plus reserved) divided by earned premium — the primary profitability signal insurers use to raise rates, tighten underwriting, or exit a line; a loss ratio over 100% means the insurer is losing money on underwriting before even counting expenses ([Quantopia: Loss Ratio in Insurance](https://www.quantopia.net/blog/loss-ratio-what-it-is-how-it-s-calculated-and-types/)).

Where liability can be shifted to a third party, the insurer can pursue **subrogation** — the right, acquired automatically upon paying a covered claim, to step into the insured's shoes and seek reimbursement from the at-fault party or their insurer ([Progressive: What Is Subrogation?](https://www.progressive.com/answers/what-is-subrogation/)). Successful subrogation often returns some or all of the insured's deductible, and some states (California among them) mandate that deductibles be included proportionally in any subrogation demand ([Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer: Under Deductible Subrogation](https://www.mwl-law.com/under-deductible-subrogation-deductible-reimbursement-helping-insureds-battle-the-cost-of-insurance/)); a **waiver of subrogation** endorsement, common in construction contracts, can block the insurer from pursuing it at all ([Construction Coverage: Waiver of Subrogation](https://constructioncoverage.com/business/waiver-of-subrogation)). Finally, the closed claim doesn't just disappear — it becomes **loss history**, aggregated in databases like **CLUE (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange)**, which insurers pull during underwriting on *new* business (claims history shifts eligibility, deductibles, and premiums) more than at renewal, where carriers typically already hold internal loss history on their own book ([InsuranceCurator: CLUE Report Basics](https://insurancecurator.com/clue-report-basics-what-it-includes-and-how-claims-history-affects-new-quotes/)) — closing the loop from a single claim back into the underwriting and rating system described earlier in this report.

## Reinsurance is insurance for insurers, structured by treaty and layer

Primary insurers buy **reinsurance** — "insurance for insurance companies" — chiefly to reduce the capital they must hold against claim liabilities, cushion against catastrophic or volatile losses, stabilize underwriting results, and expand underwriting capacity; the NAIC identifies seven distinct motivations in total, including financing operations, spreading risk, exiting lines, and acquiring specialized expertise ([NAIC CIPR: Reinsurance](https://content.naic.org/cipr_topics/topic_reinsurance.htm)). Mechanically, ceding reinsurance reduces an insurer's unearned-premium and loss reserves on its books, letting it hold a lower capital cushion while still expanding how much business it can write ([Triple-I: Background on Reinsurance](https://www.iii.org/article/background-on-reinsurance)). The single historical catalyst most cited for the modern reinsurance market's structure is **Hurricane Andrew (1992)**, whose $15.5 billion in insured losses made traditional catastrophe capacity scarce and expensive, spurring alternative instruments like catastrophe bonds, industry loss warranties, and sidecars ([Triple-I](https://www.iii.org/article/background-on-reinsurance)).

Reinsurance is placed through two fundamentally different mechanisms. **Treaty** reinsurance is a standing, automatic agreement covering an entire defined book of business (e.g., all homeowners' policies) — the reinsurer must accept every risk that fits the treaty's terms. **Facultative** reinsurance is negotiated risk by risk, with the reinsurer free to accept or reject each individual submission, typically reserved for unusually large or hazardous risks that don't fit cleanly within a standing treaty ([Insurance Business Magazine: Facultative and Treaty Reinsurance](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/guides/facultative-and-treaty-reinsurance-whats-the-difference-168929.aspx)). Orthogonal to that mechanism is *how* losses are shared. **Proportional** treaties split every premium and loss dollar by a fixed ratio: in **quota share**, cedent and reinsurer split a flat percentage of every policy (e.g., a 40% quota share means the reinsurer takes 40% of premium and pays 40% of every claim) ([Insurance Business Magazine: Quota Share](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/reinsurance/news/breaking-news/how-quota-share-reinsurance-works-in-the-global-market-547309.aspx)); in **surplus share**, the insurer keeps a fixed dollar "line" per risk and cedes only the amount above it, so the cession percentage varies policy by policy rather than staying flat — more administratively complex but far less premium ceded on smaller, safer risks ([Insurance Business Magazine: Surplus Share](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/reinsurance/news/breaking-news/what-is-surplus-share-reinsurance-an-essential-guide-for-brokers-worldwide-551318.aspx)). **Non-proportional** structures work differently still: in **excess of loss (XL)** reinsurance, the reinsurer pays only losses above a dollar **attachment point** (retention) up to a **limit**, conventionally written as "Limit xs Retention" (e.g., "$10M xs $5M" covers losses between $5M and $15M), with no interest in anything below the attachment point and no liability above the limit ([Second Order Risk: Excess of Loss Reinsurance](https://secondorderrisk.substack.com/p/excess-of-loss-reinsurance-a-practical)). **Catastrophe excess of loss** is the specialized, occurrence-based version of this that responds to the *aggregation* of many losses from one event — crucial for property lines specifically because catastrophe losses are highly correlated across a book (a single hurricane can trigger thousands of simultaneous claims), unlike auto liability losses, which are largely independent of one another ([Insurance Business Magazine: Catastrophe Reinsurance Fundamentals](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/reinsurance/news/breaking-news/the-fundamentals-of-catastrophe-reinsurance-for-us-brokers-547780.aspx)).

Whatever structure is used, reinsurance is a **business-to-business contract between the ceding insurer and the reinsurer only** — the underlying policyholder has no contractual privity with, and generally no direct legal recourse against, the reinsurer, and the primary insurer remains fully liable to its policyholders regardless of whether its own reinsurer pays ([Lexology: Contractual Privity and Reinsurance](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=82e0c3d9-4aaa-4746-81bd-de0fec63f5c5)). This is the clearest structural difference from primary insurance: a retail or commercial P&C policy is a direct contract of indemnity between insurer and insured, while reinsurance is a wholesale, capital-management transaction one layer removed from the public entirely. The market itself is concentrated: a handful of global reinsurers — Munich Re, Swiss Re, Hannover Re, SCOR, Berkshire Hathaway, and the Lloyd's of London syndicate market — dominate traditional capacity ([Insurance Journal: 40 Largest Reinsurers 2025](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2025/09/09/838096.htm)), intermediated by two dominant specialist brokers, Guy Carpenter and Aon Reinsurance Solutions ([Reinsurance News: Broker Rankings](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/aon-tops-reinsurance-broker-ranking-on-2024-revenues-but-guy-carpenter-ahead-in-2025/)), with **January 1** standing as the single largest annual treaty renewal date in the global calendar, especially for property and property-catastrophe business ([Insurance Journal: January 2024 Renewal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2024/01/03/754179.htm)).

## Fifty states, one coordinating body: how US insurance regulation actually works

US insurance regulation is organized at the **state** level almost by historical accident preserved by deliberate legislative choice. States began appointing insurance commissioners as early as 1850, decades before any federal involvement was contemplated, and by 1871 nearly every state had some form of oversight in place ([Wikipedia: McCarran-Ferguson Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarran%E2%80%93Ferguson_Act)). When the Supreme Court ruled in 1944 (*United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Association*) that insurance crossing state lines was interstate commerce subject to federal regulation — reversing 76 years of precedent — Congress responded within a year with the **McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945)**, restoring state primacy over insurance while preserving federal authority over specific anticompetitive practices like boycott, coercion, and intimidation ([Wikipedia: McCarran-Ferguson Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarran%E2%80%93Ferguson_Act)). Each state Department of Insurance (DOI) directly regulates licensing of companies, producers, adjusters, and appraisers; **market conduct** (claims handling, underwriting, sales practices — consumer-facing fairness); rate and form filing/approval; and financial/**solvency** examinations of its domestic insurers ([TDI Self-Evaluation Report](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/reports/sunset/documents/02-key-functions-performance.pdf); [NAIC CIPR: Market Conduct Regulation](https://content.naic.org/cipr-topics/market-conduct-regulation)). The practical consequence for any national carrier or PAS vendor: up to **56 distinct regulatory regimes** (50 states, DC, and five territories) for rates, forms, and licensing — a structural fragmentation with no single federal override.

Coordinating across that fragmentation is the job of the **NAIC** (National Association of Insurance Commissioners) — a private, nonprofit, non-governmental standard-setting body, founded in 1871 and governed by the states' own chief insurance regulators, with **no independent regulatory authority of its own** ([NAIC: About](https://content.naic.org/about)). States have not delegated authority to NAIC; its model laws carry no legal force until individually enacted by a state legislature or adopted by a state DOI ([LegalClarity: NAIC's Role](https://legalclarity.org/what-does-naic-stand-for-in-insurance-and-what-is-its-role/)). Its actual levers are a **Financial Regulation Standards and Accreditation Program** (created after a wave of insurer insolvencies in the 1980s, re-evaluating each state's solvency-regulation program roughly every five years), centralized data collection (insurers must annually file Risk-Based Capital reports with both their home state and the NAIC), and shared technology infrastructure — most notably **SERFF** (System for Electronic Rate and Form Filing), used by all 50 states to process hundreds of thousands of rate and form filings per year ([AgentSync: NAIC Accreditation Program](https://agentsync.io/blog/industry-news/naic-financial-regulation-standards-and-accreditation-program); [SOA: 30-Year Retrospective of RBC](https://www.soa.org/sections/financial-reporting/financial-reporting-newsletter/2025/august/fr-2025-08-cramer/)). "NAIC-compliant" is therefore best read as a loose approximation rather than a guarantee of uniform legal requirements — each state adopts model language with its own wordsmithing and deviations.

Within that state-based system, a risk can land in one of two markets. **Admitted** insurers are licensed in the state, must file and get approval for their rates, and are backed by the state **guaranty fund** if they become insolvent. **Surplus lines** (non-admitted, or E&S) insurers are not licensed in that state and operate with deregulated rates and forms — used for risks the admitted market won't or can't write: new or hard-to-place risks, higher limits than admitted carriers will offer, or emerging risks lacking the loss history for standard rating ([AgentSync: Surplus Lines 101](https://agentsync.io/blog/insurance-101/what-is-excess-and-surplus-lines-insurance)). Most states require a documented "diligent search" — commonly three declinations from admitted carriers — before a risk can go surplus, and the guaranty-fund safety net explicitly does **not** cover surplus-lines policies, the central trade-off of using that market ([Still Insurable: Admitted vs Surplus Lines](https://stillinsurable.com/learn/admitted-vs-surplus-lines/)). The 2011 Dodd-Frank-era **Nonadmitted and Reinsurance Reform Act (NRRA)** simplified this by limiting regulatory authority over any surplus-lines transaction to the insured's single "home state," rather than every state touched by the risk ([SLAI: NRRA](https://www.slai.org/docs/compliance/laws-nonadmitted-reinsurance-reform-act-nrra/)).

**Solvency regulation** underpins all of this: insurers must meet **Risk-Based Capital (RBC)** requirements — a formula-driven measure of the capital needed to absorb asset, insurance, interest-rate, and operational risk, adopted by NAIC in the mid-1990s — reported annually under conservative **Statutory Accounting Principles (SAP)**, which differ deliberately from GAAP because SAP is built for regulators assessing claims-paying ability rather than investors assessing going-concern value ([CAS: Solvency II Standard Formula and NAIC RBC](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_12fforumpt2_rbc-dcwprpt3.pdf)). If an insurer fails anyway, state **guaranty associations** step in as a last resort, funded — unlike the FDIC's pre-funded model — by **post-insolvency assessments** on the remaining solvent insurers in that state ([PlanEasy: State Guaranty Associations](https://planeasy.com/articles/how-are-state-insurance-guaranty-associations-funded)). RBC, SAP, and guaranty funds are sequential controls, not redundant ones: the first two aim to catch weak insurers early, the last is the backstop triggered only after a court-ordered liquidation.

Two further bodies round out the standardization picture, and are worth distinguishing precisely because they are often conflated. **ISO** standardizes policy *language* (forms) and *actuarial data* (loss costs) — it is the industry's shared statistical infrastructure, lowering the fixed cost for any insurer to enter a new line or state without building its own forms and actuarial base from scratch. **ACORD** standardizes the *transactional* layer — application forms, certificates, and, increasingly, the electronic/XML data standards (over 1,200 transaction types, 850+ form variants) that let agency systems, carrier systems, and rating engines talk to each other without bespoke integration for every pair of trading partners ([Wikipedia: ACORD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACORD)). Both are voluntary, market-driven standards — unlike NAIC's state-adoption model, nobody is legally required to use ISO forms or ACORD data standards, yet near-universal adoption solves what would otherwise be an N-carriers-by-M-agents coordination problem. **AM Best** closes the loop as the industry's dominant financial-strength rating agency: its forward-looking **Financial Strength Rating (FSR)**, scaled from A++ (Superior) down to D (Poor), evaluates balance-sheet strength, operating performance, business profile, and risk management, and functions as the market's day-to-day proxy for counterparty claims-paying reliability — many state regulators and consumer-protection guidance suggest an A- or better threshold — complementing rather than substituting for the regulatory solvency metrics above ([CoverRight: Understanding AM Best Ratings](https://coverright.com/knowledge/glossary/understanding-am-best-ratings/)).

## What this means for building in the space

The thread running through every section above is that P&C insurance is a system of **layered delegation and layered override**, repeated at every level: carriers delegate underwriting to MGAs who delegate referral thresholds back up; base policy forms get overridden by endorsements that take precedence; base rates get multiplied by rating factors that can be recalibrated independently; and 50 states each layer their own regulatory requirements on top of NAIC model language that has no force until adopted locally. A policy administration system that models these as flat, single-layer records — one "status" field for lifecycle stage, one flat rate for premium, one form per policy — will break the first time it meets a mid-term endorsement, a referral-triggered underwriting exception, or a state-specific cancellation rule. The second, less obvious takeaway is that the industry's standardization bodies (ISO, ACORD, NAIC, AM Best) are not competitors to a PAS or insurtech product — they are shared infrastructure that most incumbents and new entrants alike build *on top of* rather than around, because the alternative is independently re-deriving actuarial data, policy wording, data-exchange formats, and state-filing content for every one of the roughly 56 US jurisdictions a serious carrier eventually has to operate in.
